# Gap and target rules

This reference constrains **how a target is set, what a gap statement must contain, and how scores become a status**. Background, loaded on demand.

## Deriving a target

A target answers one question: **what level does this capability have to reach for a stated strategic commitment to hold?**

The derivation is written down. "The strategy commits to next-day delivery across all four regions by 2028; that requires route planning to be measured and managed rather than run on judgement, so the target is 4." That sentence is what makes the target arguable, and a target nobody can argue with is a target nobody can correct.

Where the strategy is not specific enough to derive a level from, **say so.** That is a finding about the strategy — and a more useful one than a number invented to fill the space.

## Most targets are lower than the maximum

| Position | Usual target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Commodity | 2–3 | It has to work reliably. Nothing competes on it and measurement infrastructure costs real money to sustain |
| Parity | 3 | Defined, consistent, trainable. Matching the sector is the requirement |
| Differentiating | 4–5 | Where measurement and deliberate improvement actually pay for themselves |

Over-targeting is the most expensive error in maturity work, and it is invisible because every individual target looks defensible in isolation. It generates real, fundable, well-scoped work on capabilities nobody competes on — and unlike most errors in this domain, the money gets spent before anyone notices.

The useful challenge for any target above 3: *what specifically would we do differently at that level, and what does the strategy need that requires it?* An answer describing the level rather than the difference is a target set by convention.

## A gap statement is a change, not a distance

Every gap is expressed across five dimensions:

| Dimension | The question |
|---|---|
| Process | What has to be defined, documented, or changed in how the work runs? |
| People | What skills, roles, or capacity do not currently exist? |
| Information | What has to be known, measured, or made trustworthy? |
| Technology | What has to be built, bought, or fixed? |
| Governance | Who decides what, and what oversight has to exist? |

"2 to 4" cannot be estimated, argued with, or assigned. The five-dimension breakdown makes it a backlog item — and it routinely reveals that **a gap everyone described as a technology gap is mostly a process and information gap** with a system attached to it. That reframing is one of the more valuable outputs of the method, and it is invisible in a numeric assessment.

## Status rules

Applied in this order. The first that matches wins.

| # | Status | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `insufficient_evidence` | Current or target could not be established from any evidence |
| 2 | `over_targeted` | The target exceeds what any stated strategic requirement supports |
| 3 | `not_closable_in_horizon` | Closing it takes longer than the strategy allows |
| 4 | `blocked_by_another_gap` | Another gap must close first |
| 5 | `accept_gap` | The organization has decided to accept the consequence, with a named acceptor |
| 6 | `close_now` | Strategic dependency ≥ 4 and readiness ≥ 3 — the strategy depends on it and someone could start |
| 7 | `close_next` | Everything else with a real gap |

`over_targeted` sits second deliberately. Every status below it presumes the target is right, and correcting a target costs nothing while closing a gap costs money.

## Rank by dependency, not by size

The instinct is to order the backlog by gap size. It is wrong, and expensively so: a three-level gap on a commodity capability outranks a one-level gap on the capability the strategy depends on, and the resulting plan spends its first year on the wrong things.

`strategic_dependency` carries the highest weight for this reason. `gap_size` is deliberately not the top-weighted criterion — it describes the distance, not the value of travelling it.

## An accepted gap is a decision

Record it with:

- **What consequence was accepted** — specifically, not "some risk".
- **Who accepted it** — a name, because an organization cannot accept anything.
- **When it should be reviewed** — an acceptance is made against today's strategy.

A gap that is silently absent from the backlog is indistinguishable from one that was overlooked. Two years later, when the consequence arrives, nobody can tell which happened, and the assessment gets blamed either way.

## Unowned gaps are a category, not a data problem

Capabilities with no accountable owner have systematically larger gaps, for an obvious reason: nobody has been accountable for closing them.

They are also the gaps most likely to survive to the next assessment unchanged, because a backlog item with no owner is not worked on by anybody in particular. Naming them as a group makes assigning an owner a single visible decision, which is usually the cheapest intervention in the whole backlog.

## What the assessment may not conclude

**The sequence.** Ordering gaps by priority is not a plan. Sequencing against delivery capacity, dependencies, and fixed dates is `roadmap-sequencing`, and a priority list presented as a roadmap will be committed to as one.

**The solution.** That a gap needs a technology change says nothing about whether to build or buy it.

**The business case.** That a gap is worth closing in priority terms is not the same as it paying for itself.

**That the backlog is deliverable.** Where capacity and funding were not supplied, the backlog is unconstrained — and an unconstrained backlog is always larger than what the organization can deliver.
