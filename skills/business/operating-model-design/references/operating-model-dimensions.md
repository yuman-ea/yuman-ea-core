# Operating model dimensions

This reference constrains **what has to be answered before a design is complete**. Background, loaded on demand — the method in [`SKILL.md`](../SKILL.md) is complete without it.

## The six dimensions

The frame follows the published Operating Model Canvas work of Andrew Campbell and colleagues, and the POLISM shorthand associated with it. It is **cited, not reproduced** — the dimension names below are ordinary descriptive words, and anyone wanting the underlying method should go to the source.

| Dimension | What it answers | The failure when it is skipped |
|---|---|---|
| **Work** | What gets done, at what volume and standard | The design arranges people around activities nobody has scoped |
| **Organization** | Which roles are accountable | — usually the only one answered |
| **Locations** | Where it happens, and what is genuinely location-bound | Work assumed movable that is not, discovered during transition |
| **Information** | What has to be known, by whom, when | Decisions placed away from the information they need |
| **Suppliers** | What others do for us, and how tightly coupled | The outsourced footprint is redesigned around without the contracts being read |
| **Management system** | What is measured, who decides, what escalates | The structure changes and the behaviour does not |

## Organization is designed last

Not because it matters least — because it depends on the other five.

Which roles are needed follows from the work. Where they sit follows from the locations and the information. What they are accountable for follows from the management system. Designing the boxes first fixes all of those implicitly and then discovers them one at a time as constraints.

This is also the practical reason so many reorganizations change nothing that matters. They start and end in the same dimension, which is the one people can see.

## The management system is the determinative one

If only one dimension beyond organization gets real attention, this is the one that changes outcomes.

It comprises three things:

1. **What is measured**, and who is accountable for each measure.
2. **Who decides what**, with what authority and what escalates.
3. **How performance is reviewed** — cadence, forum, and what actually happens when a measure is missed.

The reason it dominates is behavioural rather than structural. **People respond to what is measured and to who they have to explain themselves to**, far more than to where they sit on a chart. A structure designed for collaboration, measured on functional targets, produces functional behaviour — reliably, and regardless of intent.

The most useful question when designing it: *what behaviour will these measures produce that we do not want?* Every measure produces one. Naming it in advance is cheaper than discovering it.

## Information: decisions and knowledge in the same place

The test for this dimension is specific: for every significant decision, is the information required available to whoever makes it?

A "no" is a design defect, not an implementation detail. It means the decision has been placed where the authority is rather than where the knowledge is, and the usual consequences follow — decisions escalate to be made worse, or get made locally without the context and then get overturned.

This is also where the centralisation argument is usually settled in practice. Centralise the decisions whose information is naturally central; devolve the ones whose information is only available locally. Stated that way, the argument is about evidence rather than about philosophy.

## Suppliers: coupling, not just cost

Two questions per arrangement, and the second is the one omitted:

- What do they do for us, at what cost?
- **What happens if they stop?**

The second determines how much of the operating model is genuinely the organization's to design. A tightly coupled supplier whose processes are embedded in the value stream is part of the operating model whether or not they appear on the chart, and redesigning around them without reading the contract is how a design meets its first hard constraint in month four.

## Locations: what is genuinely bound

Distribution, warehousing, cold storage, delivery, and anything touching physical goods is location-bound by nature. Almost everything else is a choice, frequently a historical one that nobody has revisited.

Two cautions in both directions. Work assumed movable that is not — because of regulation, data residency, proximity to a customer, or the need to be physically present when something goes wrong — is discovered expensively during transition. And work assumed fixed that is not is the cheapest option most designs leave on the table.

## Completeness test

Before a design is presented, every dimension has an answer and the trade-off of each answer is stated. A canvas with three dimensions filled and three left generic is not an operating model; it is an organization chart with an introduction.

Where a dimension genuinely does not change — the locations are what they are, and nothing in the design touches them — say that explicitly. "Unchanged, deliberately" is a complete answer. A blank section is not, because a reader cannot tell it from an omission.
