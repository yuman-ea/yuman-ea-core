# Stakeholder rules

This reference constrains **who goes on the list and how their power is judged**. Background, loaded on demand — the method in [`SKILL.md`](../SKILL.md) is complete without it.

## Three groups, and the third decides whether this works

| Group | What they can do | Usually captured |
|---|---|---|
| **Approvers** | Stop it formally | Yes |
| **Consulted** | Shape it, and complain if not asked | Yes |
| **Affected, with no seat** | Nothing directly — and everything indirectly | **No** |

The third group is operators, support teams, customers, and suppliers. They cannot raise a concern through any channel this engagement has, so their objection arrives through somebody else — usually an approver, usually late, and usually in a distorted form that is harder to answer than the original.

Finding them takes one question: *who has to live with this once we have finished?*

## Blocking power is not interest

Four kinds, and the second is the one that gets missed:

| Kind | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `formal_veto` | Named in the governance. Their signature is required |
| `practical_veto` | Nothing proceeds without their cooperation, whatever the governance says |
| `influential` | Their view moves others, without authority of their own |
| `none` | Affected or interested, with no lever |

`practical_veto` is held by people whose authority to stop things appears in no document: the person who controls a scarce resource, the one whose team has to do the work, the long-serving manager whose scepticism ends conversations. Estimated influence maps overweight formal seniority and miss all of them, which is why `stakeholder_influence` carries an explicit assumption when it is estimated.

**The most dangerous cell in the matrix is high blocking power with no interest.** An approver in a regulated process who does not care about the content still has to sign, and a concern they have never articulated is one nobody can address in advance. They are not obstructive; they are simply not thinking about this until the day it reaches them.

## The blocker who has never been asked

A stakeholder with blocking power and no recorded concern is the single most reliable source of late failure.

Two cases, and they look identical on the map:

- **Not asked.** An oversight, and cheap to fix at kick-off.
- **Asked, did not engage.** Frequently a signal in itself — either the work is not important to them yet, or they are waiting to see what it says before committing a position.

Both are the same risk, so the map does not distinguish them in the status. The rationale should, because the responses differ: the first needs a conversation and the second needs a decision about whether to proceed without them.

## Roles, not only names

The register is a role list with names attached, not a name list.

Two reasons. It survives a departure, which matters because engagements outlast individuals more often than expected. And it makes the map reusable — the next engagement touching the same governance starts from a role register rather than from nothing.

Keep the names, though. "The Finance Director" and "the Finance Director who has been in post three weeks" are different stakeholders in practice.

## Escalation runs where it runs

Escalation paths inferred from the reporting structure are frequently wrong.

Real escalation goes to whoever owns the budget, or to the person both parties trust, or to the forum where the decision can actually be made. A conflict escalated up a reporting line that cannot resolve it does not get resolved — it stalls, quietly, and reappears at the next decision point.

Where the path was inferred, say so. Where it is known, record it, because the escalation route is the thing the conflict section depends on.

## Judging concerns is not this method's job

A concern held by someone who can stop the work is material **whether or not it is well founded.**

This is worth stating because the temptation is strong and the reasoning feels sound: a stakeholder's concern rests on a misunderstanding, so it will dissolve once explained, so it need not shape the deliverables. It routinely does not dissolve, and the deliverable that would have addressed it was never produced.

The method's job is to record what has to be answered and for whom. Whether the answer is "here is the analysis" or "here is why that risk does not apply" is a matter for the view, not for the map.

## Prior history predicts behaviour

A stakeholder who reopened a settled decision before will do it again, and it is the most reliable predictor available.

That is not a character judgement — it usually means their concern was never actually addressed the first time, only outvoted. Recording the pattern is what allows the map to give that concern more attention this time, which is generally cheaper than the alternative.

Where no history is available, the map treats everyone as approaching the work fresh, and says so.
