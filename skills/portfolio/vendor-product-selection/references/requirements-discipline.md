# Writing requirements that are yours, not the market's

Background, loaded on demand. **The method in [`SKILL.md`](../SKILL.md) is complete without this file.** Reach for it when the requirements list is being drafted, or when a selection looks decided before it has been run.

## The failure this prevents

A selection is usually decided in the first two weeks, before any scoring happens, by whoever writes the requirements. If those requirements were written after the demonstrations, they describe the products that were demonstrated — and the scorecard that follows is an elaborate confirmation of a choice already made.

Nobody does this deliberately. It happens because demonstrations are genuinely educational: you see a capability you had not thought of, it is obviously useful, and it goes on the list. By the time the list is finished it contains six features that exist in one product because that is where you learned about them.

**The tell is a must-have that only one candidate meets.** Sometimes that is a real differentiator and the right answer. Often it is a line lifted from a feature list. The two are indistinguishable in the document and completely different in what they mean — so the method asks directly: **who wrote this, and when, relative to seeing any product?**

## Must-have or want

**A must-have disqualifies.** That is the entire test. If a candidate that cannot meet it would still be considered, it is a want.

Applied honestly this usually cuts a list of thirty to a list of six or eight. Applied dishonestly — where everything is a must-have because everything feels important — every candidate fails at least one, the scorecard becomes unusable, and the decision gets made on something outside it entirely. That is worse than having no requirements, because it produces a decision nobody can reconstruct.

Wants are not lesser requirements. They are **the tradeable ones**, and stating what you would give up for what is where a selection actually gets its leverage. A wants list with a trade column is more useful in a negotiation than the must-haves are.

## Writing a requirement that can be evaluated

Four failure modes, all common:

**The feature, not the need.** "Configurable approval workflow" is a feature. "A regional manager can approve an order above the credit limit without waiting for head office" is a need — and it might be met three different ways, two of which you had not considered.

**The unmeasurable adjective.** "Intuitive", "flexible", "modern", "scalable". Every product meets these; none can fail them. Either make it observable or move it to wants and stop pretending it is being scored.

**The requirement with a product in it.** Naming a product, a format, or a specific technology inside a requirement pre-selects. Where a standard genuinely is the requirement — because partners already use it — say why, so the reasoning survives.

**The requirement nobody owns.** Every requirement needs a name attached. Unowned requirements accumulate during evaluation, are never traded away because nobody can authorize it, and quietly eliminate candidates that would have been fine.

## Non-functional requirements are where the surprise lives

Functional requirements get attention because they are what the business asks about. Non-functional requirements decide whether the thing works at your volumes, and they are usually written last, if at all.

State them **under this organization's conditions**, not the supplier's reference conditions. A product that handles ten thousand transactions an hour handles them on the supplier's benchmark hardware with their reference data model. Yours is different, and the difference is discovered in performance testing after signature.

Where they are derived rather than stated, mark them as proposals for confirmation. A product chosen against invented targets is a product chosen on features.

## Supplier requirements are requirements

Support hours, escalation commitments, roadmap transparency, contract flexibility, willingness to provide references of comparable size — these are requirements, they can be specified, and they can be scored.

They are usually left as impressions. Then the organization discovers after signature that support is business hours in a different timezone, and that this was in the response document all along, unread, because nobody had made it a requirement.

## The provenance section

The specification records who was consulted, over what period, and whether any product had been seen at the time.

That section is not bureaucracy. It is what makes the document credible six months later, when somebody asks whether the requirements were shaped by the winning product — and it is the only way to answer that question with anything other than an assurance.
