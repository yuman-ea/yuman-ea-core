# Finding options that are actually different

Background, loaded on demand. **The method in [`SKILL.md`](../SKILL.md) is complete without this file.** Reach for it when you have one obvious answer and need to know whether a genuine alternative exists.

## Why three

Not numerology. Three does something specific that two cannot.

**With one option, there is no decision** — only a rationale, and the record should say so.

**With two, the comparison collapses into a binary** and the reasoning tends to become a defence of the one already preferred. Two options invite the question "is A better than B?" when the useful question is "what kind of answer is right here?"

**With three, a dimension appears.** The third option is usually the one that reveals what the decision is actually about — that the real trade-off was between coupling and speed, not between the two technologies first named. That is the value, and it is why three is the working practice rather than a quota.

**Beyond four, returns fall off sharply** and the record becomes unreadable. Nobody has ever been thanked for a seven-option comparison.

Two is acceptable. It is capped at `medium` confidence and it must state why a third was unavailable — because sometimes there genuinely isn't one, and saying so plainly is worth more than padding.

## What does not count as an option

**Two configurations of the same thing.** Different sizing, different settings, same architecture. That is one option with a parameter.

**The straw man.** An alternative included so the preferred option wins, and recognisable because nobody can say what would make it the right choice. Every experienced reviewer spots this, and it damages the credibility of the decision it was meant to protect. **If you cannot argue for an option honestly, take it out and reduce the count.** A recorded two is more credible than a manufactured three.

**"Do nothing" written to be dismissed.** Doing nothing is a real option when it is genuinely available and scored honestly. It is not an option when it is there to be crossed out in the first paragraph.

## Where a third option usually hides

When you have two and need a real third, these produce one more often than not:

**Defer.** Keep the choice open, take the reversible path now, decide at the last responsible moment. Where the urgency answer allows it this is a genuine option, and recording that you *could* have waited and chose not to is more useful than pretending the decision was forced.

**Split the difference.** Apply one option to the part that needs it and the other elsewhere. In sourcing this is "build the differentiating part, buy the rest"; in a technology choice it is usually a boundary you had not thought to draw.

**Change the constraint instead of the solution.** Two options both look bad because a requirement is forcing them. Sometimes the third option is negotiating the requirement — and if it turns out to be non-negotiable, you have learned that too, and the record is stronger for it.

**Buy time rather than solve it.** A deliberate, time-boxed stopgap with a recorded expiry is a legitimate option. It becomes a problem only when it is undeclared, which is exactly what a decision record prevents.

**Ask what a team with different constraints would do.** More people, fewer people, no existing estate, a stricter regulator. The answer is frequently viable for you too, with an adjustment.

## Framing the option set well

**Options should differ in kind, not in degree.** "Broker A, broker B, broker C" is a product shortlist, not an architecture decision — and product selection is a different question that comes after this one.

**Name each option by what it does, not what it uses.** "Publish events and let consumers read at their own pace" survives a change of technology. "Use {product}" does not, and it pre-empts a selection decision that has not been taken.

**Describe each in one sentence a non-specialist could picture.** If you cannot, either you do not understand the option yet or it is really two options.

## When there is genuinely only one

It happens, and it is not a failure. A regulator permits one approach; a contract locks a component; only one thing in the estate can do the job at all.

Say so. Record why each alternative was not viable — that list is the most valuable content in a single-option record, because it is exactly what a future reader will want when the constraint lifts. Mark the record as a rationale record, rate the run `low`, and move on.

**What you must not do is fill the table.** The temptation is strongest here, because a single-option record feels weak — and a manufactured comparison feels rigorous. The reverse is true. An honest single-option record with three well-explained non-viable alternatives is a stronger document than a three-option comparison anybody can see through.
